Companion planting means growing different vegetables, herbs and flowers side by side so they complement rather than hinder each other. Classic good neighbours include tomatoes and basil, carrots and onions, or cabbage and celery; poor neighbours include beans and onions, or tomatoes and potatoes. Combined with a simple crop rotation, it keeps your soil healthy and your harvests reliable.

## Why companion planting makes sense

Long rows of a single crop make life easy for pests and diseases. A mixed bed works differently:

- **Better use of space:** Deep- and shallow-rooted, tall and low plants use soil and light in different ways.
- **Less pest pressure:** Pests that find their host by scent have a harder time in a mixed bed. Aromatic herbs can help.
- **Nutrients complement each other:** Beans and peas fix nitrogen from the air with the help of root nodule bacteria.
- **More beneficial insects:** Flowering herbs and flowers like calendula, nasturtium or phacelia attract hoverflies, lacewings and wild bees.
- **Covered soil:** Densely planted beds dry out less and have fewer weeds.

To be honest, many pairing rules are based on decades of gardening experience rather than controlled trials. The core principles, though, such as diversity, rotating plant families and mixing root depths, are widely accepted.

## Companion planting chart: good and bad neighbours

### The classics in detail

**Tomato and basil:** Both love warmth and sun and have similar water and nutrient needs. Basil does well beneath tomatoes, where it is slightly sheltered.

**Carrot and onion:** This pairing is the textbook example. The smell of onions is said to confuse carrot fly, and the smell of carrots to confuse onion fly. How strong the effect is depends on conditions; insect mesh remains the most reliable protection against carrot fly.

**Cabbage and celery:** Celery is said to deter cabbage white butterflies. Both also share space well, as celery grows more upright.

**Beans and summer savory:** Savory is traditionally planted to keep black bean aphids away, and it goes well with beans in the kitchen too.

### Why some plants don't get along

- **Same family, same problems:** Tomatoes and potatoes are both nightshades and both suffer from late blight. Planted side by side, they easily infect each other.
- **Competition for nutrients:** Two heavy feeders right next to each other, such as squash and potatoes, compete hard.
- **Root exudates:** Onion-family plants are thought to inhibit the growth of legumes. This observation comes from gardening practice.
- **Different needs:** Basil needs plenty of water, thyme likes it dry. Planted together, one of them suffers.

## Herbs and flowers as helpers

Companion planting is not only about vegetables next to vegetables. Herbs and flowers play important roles in the bed:

- **Calendula (pot marigold):** loosens the soil with its roots and attracts beneficial insects such as hoverflies, whose larvae eat aphids.
- **Nasturtium:** draws aphids to itself and can take pressure off other plants. Its flowers and leaves are edible.
- **French marigold (Tagetes):** traditionally planted against certain soil nematodes. Be aware that slugs love it.
- **Dill and coriander:** their umbrella-shaped flowers attract beneficial insects, and they pair well with carrots, cucumbers and cabbages.
- **Borage:** an excellent bee plant that helps pollinate cucumbers, courgettes and squash.

Plan one or two of these flowering plants per bed, ideally along the edges or in corners where they will not shade the vegetables.

## Companion planting in raised beds and on balconies

Space in a raised bed is limited, which makes companion planting especially worthwhile. An example for a 1.2 × 2 m raised bed in its first year:

- **Centre:** tomatoes or courgettes (heavy feeders, need space)
- **Around them:** kohlrabi, lettuce, basil
- **Edges:** radishes, carrots, onions, and nasturtiums trailing over the side

The same principle works on a balcony: tomato with basil in a container, strawberries with chives in a window box, lettuce with radishes.

## Crop rotation: move things around each year

Companion planting decides what grows **side by side**. Crop rotation decides what grows **one after another** in the same spot. Together they keep your soil healthy.

### The basic rule

Plants of the same family should only return to the same spot after three to four years. This matters especially for:

- **Brassicas** (cabbage, radish, rocket): risk of clubroot, a persistent soil disease
- **Nightshades** (tomato, potato, pepper, aubergine)
- **Alliums** (onion, garlic, leek)
- **Umbellifers** (carrot, celery, parsnip, parsley, dill)

### A simple four-bed rotation

Divide your vegetable garden into four areas and move each group along by one bed every year:

| Year | Bed 1 | Bed 2 | Bed 3 | Bed 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Heavy feeders | Medium feeders | Light feeders | Green manure / rest |
| 2 | Green manure | Heavy feeders | Medium feeders | Light feeders |
| 3 | Light feeders | Green manure | Heavy feeders | Medium feeders |
| 4 | Medium feeders | Light feeders | Green manure | Heavy feeders |

- **Heavy feeders:** tomato, cabbage, squash, courgette, cucumber, potato, leek, celery
- **Medium feeders:** carrot, beetroot, lettuce, spinach, chard, onion, fennel
- **Light feeders:** bean, pea, radish, lamb's lettuce, many herbs

If you only have one bed, you can rotate the groups within it over the years. The most important thing is not to grow tomatoes, potatoes and cabbages in the same spot every year. How much feed each group needs is explained in our guide on [how to fertilize plants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-plants/).

## Catch crops and succession sowing

Besides companion planting and rotation, you can also make better use of a bed over time. Fast crops like radishes, spinach or cut-and-come-again lettuce work well before tomatoes or beans, which only go out in mid-May. After early potatoes or peas, there is room for follow-on crops such as lamb's lettuce, winter lettuce or kale. Our [sowing calendar](/en/blog/sowing-calendar/) shows what to sow when.
