Most vegetable beds need one or two deep waterings per week in dry weather, roughly 10 to 20 litres per square metre, while containers and window boxes often need water every day in midsummer. There is no single watering schedule that fits every plant: pot size, soil, weather and plant type all matter. With the finger test and a few rules of thumb, though, you will quickly find the right rhythm.

## The golden rule: water less often, but deeply

Many gardeners give their plants a small splash every day. The trouble is that the water only reaches the top layer of soil, roots stay shallow and plants become more sensitive to heat. It is better to water less often but thoroughly, so the water soaks 15 to 20 cm into the ground. Roots follow the moisture downwards and can still find water there on hot days.

A few basics that almost always apply:

- **Water in the morning:** Little water evaporates early in the day, and the foliage dries off quickly. Evening watering is the second-best option but encourages slugs and fungal problems.
- **Water the roots, not the leaves:** Tomatoes, cucumbers and courgettes in particular respond to wet foliage with mildew and blight.
- **Use the finger test, not the calendar:** Push a finger 2 to 3 cm into the soil. If it feels dry at that depth, water.
- **Use rainwater or water at air temperature:** Water that has stood in the can for a few hours is gentler than ice-cold water from the hose.

## What affects how much water a plant needs

### Pot size and material

The smaller the container, the faster it dries out. A 5-litre pot with a tomato plant can dry out within a day in July, while a 40-litre tub lasts much longer. Clay and terracotta pots lose extra moisture through their walls and dry out faster than plastic. Dark pots heat up considerably in the sun.

### Soil and potting mix

Sandy soils drain quickly and need watering more often, in smaller amounts. Clay soils hold water for a long time but do not tolerate being waterlogged. A mulch of grass clippings, straw or leaves keeps moisture in the bed and saves a lot of work. In pots, a good-quality container compost holds water better than cheap, peat-heavy mixes, which are hard to re-wet once they have dried out.

### Weather and season

Sun, wind and heat increase water demand enormously. In April and May rain is often enough; in July and August you will need to help out regularly. A proper summer downpour usually replaces one watering, but a short shower does not: under dense foliage and in window boxes beneath roof overhangs, very little actually reaches the soil. Demand drops noticeably in autumn, and overwintering container plants need only a little water.

### Plant type

Fruiting vegetables such as tomatoes, cucumbers, courgettes and squash are thirsty, especially once fruit starts to form. Mediterranean herbs like rosemary, thyme and sage get by with little water and hate wet roots. Lettuce and brassicas need evenly moist soil, or they bolt or turn bitter.

## Watering schedule at a glance

The table below gives guideline values for a Central European summer. It does not replace the finger test, but it helps with planning.

| Plant / location | Frequency in summer | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Vegetable bed, general | 1–2× per week, 10–20 l/m² | Water deeply, mulch |
| Tomatoes in the ground | every 2–3 days | Keep it even, or fruit splits |
| Tomatoes in containers | daily, twice in heatwaves | Use at least 20 l pots |
| Cucumbers, courgettes | every 1–2 days | Lots of water once fruit sets |
| Lettuce, kohlrabi | every 2–3 days | Even moisture prevents bolting |
| Mediterranean herbs | once a week or less | Let the soil dry between waterings |
| Basil, parsley | every 1–2 days in pots | Avoid wetting the leaves |
| Window boxes (sunny) | daily | Check morning and evening in heat |
| Large containers | every 1–3 days | Depends on pot size and sun |

## Vegetables, herbs and balcony plants in detail

### Vegetables in beds

Freshly sown rows and young transplants need regular water for the first two to three weeks because their roots are still short. After that, switch to less frequent, generous waterings. Tomatoes respond to irregular watering with split fruit and blossom end rot. A small soil ring around the plant, or a flower pot sunk next to the stem, helps direct the water straight to the roots.

### Herbs

With herbs, it pays to think about where they come from. Rosemary, thyme, lavender and oregano are native to dry regions and rot quickly in soggy soil. Chives, parsley, mint and basil prefer even moisture. If you grow herbs in one shared box, keep the thirsty ones and the frugal ones apart.

### Balcony and container plants

For pots, water until it runs out of the drainage hole, then empty the saucer after about 15 to 30 minutes. Standing water in the saucer leads to root rot. A drainage layer of expanded clay or broken crocks and a drainage hole are essential. Self-watering containers stretch the intervals but do not replace regular checks.

## Too much or too little water? The signs

The symptoms look similar, because the plant wilts in both cases. The key is how the soil feels.

| Sign | Underwatering | Overwatering |
|---|---|---|
| Soil | dry, pulls away from the pot edge | constantly wet, may smell musty |
| Leaves | limp, curled, dry brown edges | yellow, soft, dropping |
| Wilting | recovers quickly after watering | stays wilted despite wet soil |
| Roots | dry but pale | brown, mushy, rotten |
| Other | flowers and fruit drop | fungus gnats, mould on the surface |

For drought stress, water generously; for a pot that has dried out completely, soak it in a bucket until no more air bubbles rise. For waterlogging, the only cure is to let it drain, wait and repot if necessary. Note that over-fertilising can also cause symptoms that look like drought. ## Watering while you are on holiday

For a few days away, simple tricks are often enough:

1. **Water thoroughly before you leave** and move pots into partial shade, where far less water evaporates.
2. **Group pots together:** Plants standing close to each other shade one another and keep humidity higher.
3. **Mulch:** A layer of mulch on beds and containers noticeably reduces evaporation.
4. **Clay spikes or bottle watering:** Clay spikes connected to a water reservoir release water slowly. Upturned plastic bottles with a watering spike work for single pots.
5. **Wick watering:** A cotton cord draws water from a bucket into the pot. Test it at home first.
6. **Drip irrigation with a timer:** The most reliable option for longer trips and many pots. Let it run for a few days before you leave.

For two weeks or more in high summer, a neighbour with a watering can is often still the safest solution. A written watering schedule listing the thirstiest plants helps a lot.

## Watering through the year

- **Spring:** Keep seedlings and young plants evenly moist; established plants often get by on rain.
- **Summer:** Main watering season. Water in the morning, mulch, and check containers daily.
- **Autumn:** Reduce watering, and do not soak container plants just before moving them indoors.
- **Winter:** Water evergreens outdoors on frost-free days during dry spells; give overwintering containers just enough to keep the root ball from drying out.
