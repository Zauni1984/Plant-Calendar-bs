When to prune fruit trees depends on the type: apples and pears mainly in late winter, between late January and March, while cherries and plums are better pruned in summer, straight after harvest. The goal is an open, airy crown that lets in sunlight and keeps producing fruiting wood. With a few basic cuts and the right tools, beginners can do this too.

## Why prune fruit trees at all?

An unpruned fruit tree grows dense, branches compete for light and the fruit stays small. Regular pruning has several benefits:

- **More light and air** in the crown, giving better-coloured fruit and fewer fungal diseases
- **A strong framework** that can carry a heavy crop
- **More even yields**, because growth and fruiting stay in balance
- **Easier harvesting**, because the tree does not shoot uncontrollably upwards

## Winter pruning or summer pruning?

Timing determines how the tree responds. This is the single most important principle of fruit tree pruning.

### Winter pruning (late January to March)

In winter the tree is dormant and has stored its reserves in the roots and trunk. Pruning now means that energy is shared among fewer buds in spring, so the tree responds with strong new growth. Winter pruning is therefore suited to shaping young trees, boosting weak trees and thinning out older ones. Only prune on frost-free days; in hard frost the wood can split at the cut and heal poorly.

### Summer pruning (June to August)

In summer the tree is in leaf and building reserves. Pruning now removes leaf area and slows growth. Summer pruning is ideal for vigorous trees, for removing upright water shoots and for stone fruit. Wounds also heal faster during the growing season. In hot weather, avoid suddenly exposing fruit and bark to full sun.

## When to prune which fruit

| Fruit | Main pruning | Additional | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apple | late January to March | summer prune July/August | Fruits on two-year-old and older wood |
| Pear | late January to March | summer prune July/August | Grows upright; train branches more horizontally |
| Sweet cherry | right after harvest (summer) | – | Avoid winter pruning because of gummosis risk |
| Sour cherry | right after harvest | – | Fruits on one-year-old wood; cut back spent shoots |
| Plum, damson | after harvest or late summer | light spring prune possible | Tends to a dense crown; thin regularly |
| Peach | spring, at or just after flowering | – | Fruits on one-year-old wood; prune every year |

These periods are guidelines for Central Europe. In colder areas, winter pruning shifts towards March.

## The key cuts

### Thinning cuts

A thinning cut removes an entire branch at its point of origin. Candidates include:

- dead, diseased or damaged branches
- branches growing inwards or crossing and rubbing against each other
- vertical water shoots
- competing leaders that rival the central leader

Cut just outside the **branch collar**, the slightly swollen ring at the base of the branch. This collar contains the tissue that closes the wound. Do not cut flush with the trunk, and do not leave stubs.

### Cutting back to a side branch

Here you shorten a branch back to a lower, outward-facing side branch, which then takes over as the leader. This lets you shorten a branch or change its direction without triggering a mass of new shoots at the cut. The remaining side branch should be at least a third as thick as the part you remove.

### Heading back

Heading back means cutting a shoot just above a bud, ideally an outward-facing one. Cut about 5 mm above the bud at a slight angle. This stimulates the buds below to sprout and is mainly used when training young trees. If you head back lots of shoots, you quickly get dense "brooms" of new growth.

### Removing large branches

Never cut a heavy branch in one go, or the bark will tear. First saw an undercut about 20 to 30 cm from the trunk, then cut through from above a little further out. Finally, remove the remaining stub cleanly at the branch collar.

## The right tools

- **Bypass secateurs** for shoots up to about 2 cm. They cut like scissors and crush less than anvil pruners.
- **Loppers** with long handles for branches up to about 4 cm.
- **Pruning saw** (folding or bow saw) for thicker branches.
- **A stable ladder**, ideally a tripod orchard ladder.

Keep your tools sharp and clean. Wipe the blades between trees, especially after cutting diseased wood, for example with alcohol.

## Common pruning mistakes

1. **Removing too much at once:** Hard pruning triggers a flood of water shoots. As a rule of thumb, remove no more than about a quarter to a third of the crown per year.
2. **Leaving stubs:** Stubs die back and can let rot into the trunk.
3. **Cutting too close to the trunk:** Removing the branch collar prevents the wound from healing.
4. **Heading back every shoot tip:** This creates dense brooms instead of an open crown.
5. **Pruning in hard frost:** The wood becomes brittle and cuts heal poorly.
6. **Pruning stone fruit in winter:** For cherries, winter pruning raises the risk of gummosis and infection.

Wound sealants are generally no longer recommended for ordinary pruning cuts, because moisture and pathogens can get trapped underneath. For very large wounds, sealing just the edge can make sense.

## A quick note on pollination partners

Many fruit trees only crop well if a compatible pollination partner that flowers at the same time grows nearby:

- **Apples and pears:** Most varieties are not sufficiently self-fertile and need a different variety as a pollen donor. Some apples, such as 'Bramley's Seedling' or 'Boskoop', produce little viable pollen and are poor pollinators for others.
- **Sweet cherries:** Usually need a pollination partner, although self-fertile varieties exist.
- **Sour cherries and many plums:** Often self-fertile, but frequently crop better with a partner.

In residential areas, suitable trees often already grow in neighbouring gardens. When buying, check the variety description or ask at the nursery.
