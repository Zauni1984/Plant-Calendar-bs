Fertilize plants during the growing season from spring to late summer and match the amount to their needs: feed hungry crops like tomatoes regularly, herbs and light feeders only sparingly. What matters most is the right balance of the main nutrients nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium (NPK) and a cautious dose, because too much fertilizer often does more harm than too little.

## NPK explained simply

Almost every fertilizer pack shows three numbers, for example 7-3-6. They give the percentage of the three main nutrients:

- **N – Nitrogen:** drives leaf and shoot growth and gives deep green foliage. Too much makes plants soft and vulnerable.
- **P – Phosphorus:** important for root development, flowers and fruit. Many garden soils already contain plenty.
- **K – Potassium:** strengthens plant tissue, improves tolerance of drought and frost, and affects the flavour and keeping quality of fruit.

Plants also need magnesium, calcium, sulphur and trace elements such as iron. A good complete fertilizer contains small amounts of these.

## Growth fertilizer or bloom fertilizer?

Many brands offer two versions with different nutrient ratios:

| Fertilizer type | Typical ratio | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Growth fertilizer | nitrogen-rich (high N) | Young plants, leafy greens, lawns, foliage plants |
| Bloom and fruit fertilizer | potassium-rich, often higher P | Tomatoes, peppers, flowering plants from flower and fruit set |
| Complete / all-purpose | balanced | Mixed beds, window boxes, if you only want one product |

A simple rule of thumb: feed with more nitrogen during the growth phase, then switch to more potassium once flowers form. If you only want to buy one product, a balanced vegetable or all-purpose fertilizer works well.

## Organic or mineral?

### Organic fertilizers

Compost, horn and hoof meal, sheep's wool pellets, well-rotted manure and organic liquid feeds have to be broken down by soil organisms first. That makes them slower but longer-lasting and gentler. They also improve soil structure and feed soil life. The risk of over-fertilizing is lower, though not zero.

### Mineral fertilizers

Mineral (synthetic) fertilizers provide nutrients in an immediately available form. They work fast and can be dosed precisely, but they are easier to overdo. Excess nutrients, especially nitrogen, can be washed out and end up in groundwater. Many home vegetable gardeners therefore rely mainly on organic fertilizers and use mineral ones only where needed.

### What works where?

- **Beds:** Compost in spring as a base, plus horn meal for hungry crops.
- **Containers and balconies:** Liquid fertilizer, because potting compost only holds limited nutrients. Alternatively, a slow-release fertilizer at planting time.
- **Herbs:** Mediterranean herbs need hardly any feed; basil and chives need a little more.

## Getting the dose right

The most important rule: **follow the label and, if in doubt, use a little less.** Liquid fertilizers are often dosed at a few millilitres per litre of water, but the exact amount varies considerably from product to product.

Practical tips:

1. **Use a measuring cup or syringe** instead of guessing.
2. **Only fertilize moist soil.** On dry roots, fertilizer acts like salt. Water with plain water first.
3. **Seedlings and young plants** get half the dose or none at first. Seed compost is deliberately low in nutrients; after pricking out, ordinary potting compost usually lasts a few weeks.
4. **Freshly potted plants** in pre-fertilized compost usually need no extra feed for the first four to six weeks.
5. **Keep a record:** Note when you fed, with what and how much. That way you can see what works.

## A seasonal feeding plan

The following rhythm works well in Central Europe. It applies to vegetables and balcony plants; fruit trees and perennials often need just one feed in spring.

| Period | Beds | Containers and balconies |
|---|---|---|
| March / April | Work in compost, horn meal for hungry crops | Repot into fresh compost, no feeding yet |
| May / June | Top-dress hungry crops | From about 4–6 weeks after planting, liquid feed weekly to fortnightly |
| July / August | Potassium-rich feed for fruiting crops | Keep feeding regularly; tomato feed for tomatoes |
| Late August / September | Taper off feeding | Stop feeding woody and container plants so shoots can harden off |
| October to February | Green manure or mulch | No feeding, except houseplants in active growth |

### Heavy, medium and light feeders

- **Heavy feeders:** tomatoes, cucumbers, squash, courgettes, cabbages, celery, leeks.
- **Medium feeders:** carrots, beetroot, lettuce, spinach, chard, fennel, onions.
- **Light feeders:** beans, peas, radishes, lamb's lettuce, many herbs. Beans and peas even fix their own nitrogen with the help of root nodule bacteria.

This grouping is also the basis for crop rotation. Find out more in our guide to [companion planting](/en/blog/companion-planting/).

## Recognising and fixing over-fertilizing

Typical signs:

- brown, crispy leaf tips and margins
- white salt crusts on the soil surface or pot rim
- wilting despite moist soil, because the roots are damaged
- soft, very fast growth with thin shoots that attracts aphids
- lots of leaves but few flowers or fruit (often too much nitrogen)

**What to do:** Flush container plants thoroughly with plain water so plenty drains out of the bottom and carries excess salts with it. If the damage is severe, repot into fresh compost. Then hold off feeding for several weeks. In beds, water generously and skip further feeding for the rest of the season.

## Homemade fertilizers

### Nettle tea (nettle manure)

Nettle tea is a classic, nitrogen-rich liquid feed you can make from your own garden.

1. Roughly chop about 1 kg of fresh nettles (without seeds).
2. Cover with about 10 litres of rainwater in a plastic or wooden container; avoid metal.
3. Cover loosely (a mesh or cloth so air can get in) and place it somewhere sunny.
4. Stir daily. After about one to two weeks it stops foaming and is ready.
5. Dilute about **1:10** with water for watering and apply around the roots.

It smells strong. A handful of rock dust can reduce the odour. It suits hungry crops like tomatoes and cabbages; onions, beans and peas do not really need it.

### Compost tea

For compost tea, put a shovelful of mature compost into a bucket of water, stir well and leave it for one to three days. Strain and water undiluted or slightly diluted. Compost tea is a mild feed and works well for young plants and window boxes.

### Other home options

- **Coffee grounds:** work small amounts into the soil for a little nitrogen. Do not spread them as a thick layer, as they can turn mouldy.
- **Horn and hoof meal:** not homemade, but a proven organic slow-release nitrogen source.
- **Green manure:** sow phacelia, mustard or clover after harvest and dig it in during autumn or spring.

Fed plants also need even watering, because nutrients can only be taken up in moist soil. Our [watering schedule guide](/en/blog/watering-schedule/) explains how often to water.
